01

Our mission

To work collaboratively in acquiring and applying the knowledge, skills, and values necessary for effective community health nursing practice. We aim to promote evidence-based learning, strengthen our understanding of family and community health needs, and develop a sense of responsibility, compassion, and dedication in providing quality nursing care.

02

Our vision

To become a team of competent, compassionate, and socially responsible future nurses who are committed to promoting health, preventing diseases, and improving the well-being of individuals, families, and communities through collaborative and evidence-based nursing practice.

03

Our learning goals

As a group, we aim to: 1.Develop a comprehensive understanding of the fundamental concepts, principles, and responsibilities of Community Health Nursing. 2.Strengthen our ability to assess family and community health needs and identify existing health concerns. 3.Apply the Family Nursing Process in developing appropriate and effective nursing interventions. 4.Apply the Family Nursing Process in developing appropriate and effective nursing interventions. 5.Enhance our communication, teamwork, collaboration, and critical-thinking skills through various learning activities. 6.Recognize the importance of health promotion, disease prevention, and community participation in improving health outcomes. 7.Develop professional values, compassion, accountability, and a commitment to continuous learning as future community health nurses.

Member 01

Lao, Frederich M.

My learning summary

If there is one thing Community Health Nursing I has taught me, it is that healthcare does not begin and end within the four walls of a hospital; sometimes, its greatest impact begins at the doorstep of a family. This course broadened my understanding of nursing by showing me that behind every health concern is a person shaped by a family, a community, and circumstances that are not always visible in a clinical setting. I learned that being a nurse means looking beyond diseases and recognizing the social, economic, cultural, and environmental realities that influence people's health. More than acquiring knowledge, I began to understand the responsibility that comes with being entrusted with the well-being of others. The Family Nursing Process taught me that meaningful care must begin with understanding. Through systematic assessment, identification of health problems, planning, implementation, and evaluation, nurses can develop interventions that address the actual needs and priorities of families rather than simply impose solutions. Meanwhile, evidence-based practice ensures that these interventions are guided by credible research, sound clinical judgment, and the family's unique circumstances. Together, these approaches transform nursing care from mere instruction into purposeful action; care that is appropriate, attainable, and sustainable. Moving forward, I want to become a community health nurse who does not simply recognize what a community lacks, but also sees the strengths, resources, and potential it already possesses. I want to be someone who listens without judgment, educates with compassion, and advocates for those whose access to healthcare remains limited. Because to me, the true measure of nursing is not how many patients we treat, but how many lives we empower to live healthier, safer, and more dignified lives. And if I have learned anything from CHN I, it is that we do not simply bring healthcare to the community; we help build a community where health becomes a right that people can understand, access, and sustain—not a privilege that only a few can afford.

Member 02

Lumbre, Dhanilo Justin C.

My learning summary

Before taking Community Health Nursing I, I pictured nursing as something that happened inside hospitals, with patients in beds, monitors beeping, and orders waiting to be carried out. I believed that if a person was sick, they would come to us, and our task was to treat them. This course changed that picture. I learned that many health problems begin long before a person ever reaches a clinic, and that some never reach one at all. They quietly develop in kitchens, in streets, in water sources, and in daily habits shaped by what a family can afford and what they have been taught to believe. I realized that nursing does not only wait for illness to arrive; it goes out to meet people where they live. I also gained a new appreciation for primary health care and the people who make it work. Barangay health workers, midwives, and volunteers carry a quiet responsibility. They know the households, remember who missed a checkup, and are often the first to notice when something is wrong. Learning about prevention, immunization, maternal and child health, and early detection helped me see that the most effective care is often the care given earliest. A vaccine given on time or a danger sign recognized early can spare a family months of suffering and expense. It taught me that prevention may not be dramatic, but it is among the most powerful things a nurse can offer. In the end, this course reminded me that a nurse's responsibility extends beyond the walls of a hospital and beyond the person in front of us. Community health nursing asks us to be observant, collaborative, compassionate, and persistent, even when progress is slow. As I continue my studies and look toward serving communities of my own, I hope to remember that health is built together, through partnerships with families, with local leaders, and with fellow health workers. I want to become a nurse who walks into a community not as someone with all the answers, but as someone willing to learn, to serve, and to stay. Because lasting change rarely arrives all at once. It grows from small, consistent acts of care, shared with the people who need them, and carried forward by the communities themselves.

Member 09

Punsalan, Marielle M.

My learning summary

Before taking Community Health Nursing I, I understood nursing primarily through the care given to a patient, the illness to be addressed, the symptoms to be managed, and the interventions needed for recovery. However, throughout this course, I began to understand that health is rarely an individual experience. Behind a patient's condition is a family whose routines may be disrupted, whose resources may be stretched, whose decisions may be influenced by limited knowledge, and whose members may be carrying worries they do not always know how to express. I realized that when one person becomes ill, the effects do not necessarily end with that person. Sometimes, an entire household learns to adjust, sacrifice, and live around an illness. This changed how I understand the responsibility of a community health nurse: we do not simply care for the person who is sick; we must also learn to see the people, circumstances, and environment that shape that person's health. One of the most meaningful things I learned was the importance of the Family Nursing Process. It taught me that providing appropriate care begins long before an intervention is implemented. We must first understand the family, its structure, relationships, health practices, beliefs, available resources, and existing concerns. Through assessment, we identify not only the problems that need attention but also the strengths that can help a family overcome them. Nursing diagnoses allow us to recognize and prioritize their health concerns, while planning and implementation guide us in developing interventions that are realistic, appropriate, and responsive to their needs. Evaluation, meanwhile, reminds us that our responsibility does not end once an intervention has been carried out. We must determine whether it has actually helped and whether further action is necessary. This process made me realize that families should never be treated as passive recipients of instructions. They must be heard, involved, respected, and empowered to participate in decisions concerning their own health. I also developed a deeper appreciation for evidence-based practice. Good intentions are important in nursing, but they are not enough. An intervention may appear helpful, and advice may be given with the sincerest concern, but we must still ask whether it is safe, appropriate, and supported by reliable evidence. Through our discussions and activities on research appraisal and evidence-based community health practices, I learned that nursing decisions should be guided by credible research, professional knowledge, and an understanding of the actual circumstances of the people receiving care. This is especially important in communities where access to healthcare, financial limitations, health beliefs, and misinformation may influence a family's ability to maintain its health. Evidence-based practice allows us to provide more than familiar advice or assumptions; it gives us a stronger foundation for recommending interventions that can make a meaningful difference. At the same time, evidence must be applied with consideration for the family's individual needs and available resources. What works in one setting may not be immediately practical in another. I also came to appreciate that health promotion and disease prevention are not always found in major interventions or dramatic improvements. Sometimes, their impact begins with a parent understanding the importance of completing a child's immunization, a family recognizing an unhealthy practice, a barangay health worker providing appropriate guidance, or a household learning how to manage an existing condition. These may seem like small contributions, but their effects can extend beyond one person and influence the health of an entire family and community. I learned that community health nursing requires patience, collaboration, continuous education, proper documentation, and an understanding of the realities that people face. It also requires us to approach individuals and families without immediately judging their decisions, because what may appear to be neglect or noncompliance could be rooted in circumstances we have not yet taken the time to understand. Above all, this course reminded me that becoming a nurse is not simply about knowing what should be done. It is also about understanding whom we are doing it for and whether our actions genuinely make their lives better. The Family Nursing Process gives us a systematic way to understand, address, and evaluate the health needs of families, while evidence-based practice ensures that the care we provide is informed, safe, and effective. Together, they teach us to approach nursing with both competence and compassion. As I continue my journey as a nursing student and eventually become a community health nurse, I hope to carry this understanding into every family I encounter. I want to be the kind of nurse who does not simply see a diagnosis, a checklist, or a health problem to be resolved, but recognizes the people behind them, their struggles, their capabilities, their fears, and their hopes for a healthier life. Because sometimes, improving a family's health does not begin with changing everything about their lives. It begins with taking the time to understand what their lives are already like, meeting them where they are, and helping them realize that better health is something they can work toward, one decision and one act of care at a time.
